The original discovery

In 1929, German-American psychologist Wolfgang Köhler showed people two abstract shapes — one spiky and angular, one rounded and smooth — and asked which was called "takete" and which was "baluba." Nearly everyone agreed: the jagged shape was takete; the rounded one was baluba.

Kiki and Bouba

The experiment was famously replicated in 2001 by neuroscientist V.S. Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard using the words "kiki" and "bouba." The result was striking: 95–98% of participants, across cultures and languages, consistently matched "kiki" to the spiky shape and "bouba" to the rounded blob. This wasn't random — it held true for speakers of English, Tamil, Hindi, and many others.

Why it happens

The leading theory involves cross-modal association — the way the mouth and tongue move to pronounce each word mirrors the visual shape. Saying "kiki" involves sharp, quick movements of the tongue against the palate. "Bouba" requires smooth, rounded lip and mouth movements. The brain links these physical sensations to the shapes we see, creating a sound-shape bridge that transcends language.

What this test adds

The classic kiki/bouba experiment tests perception. This test extends the framework into personality — asking whether your inner world feels more like the sharpness of kiki or the softness of bouba. Do you move through life with angular precision or rounded adaptability? Both are strengths. Neither is better. The point is awareness.
